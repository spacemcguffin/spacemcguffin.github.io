Keeping Things Fresh (Up yours, BBC!)

Monday, 2 June 2014
Come along, Donut, what're you waiting for? We've got the whole of Iceworld to explore, then home in time for tea! No prizes for guessing which story I've just watched. Dragonfire, Season 24's finale, a cool three parter written by Ian Briggs. And it's nowhere near as bad as some would have you believe. No, really.

I mean, it looks cheap, yes. The direction by Chris Clough is pedestrian. The sets are a bit flimsy. The dialogue, in parts, is downright awful (ref: basically everything Ace says). But it's still great. It's overflowing with potential, brimming with ideas, and is jolly good fun from start to finish. There's a treasure map quest, the return of Glitz, a literal cliffhanger and a brilliantly chilling villain in the form of Kane (Edward Peel). Maybe it's just me who overlooks the flaws of Season 24, I dunno, but I really do enjoy it and I love this season finale.

Sylvester's really hit his stride as the Doctor and glimpses of his more serious persona start to shine through, whilst also having a whale of a time. The clown settles down. He's the only one who remembers they're on Iceworld too, slipping around the place whilst the rest of the actors just walk as normal - breaks the effect, but kudos to Sylv for going the extra mile with the finer details. His companion Mel, played by Bonnie, also gets some time to shine, particularly with new girl Ace. Bonnie and Sophie gel and work wonders together - just watch the scene where Ace reveals her real name - and it's a shame we didn't get another story with the two of them together. (Mel in Remembrance of the Daleks - I'll leave you to ponder that one...)

The supporting cast are pretty decent too. As noted, Edward Peel is fantastic, really selling the character and the situation he finds himself in (even if he does have to talk about how perfect his ice statue is when it's bloody awful!), Patricia Quinn as Belazs is great, and Tony Selby puts in a good performance too as Sabalabalabalom Glitz. He doesn't ever feel out of place, and frankly it's nice to have a small bit of continuity between this and Colin Baker's era so good on JNT for suggesting that Glitz return. Sophie Aldred is also a breath of fresh air, even though she gets perhaps the most unnatural dialogue in the history of dialogue, and is great fun to watch as she waltzes around being rebellious and blowing shit up.

The Xenomorph Dragon isn't the best alien design ever, but it's passable and isn't your average monster for a change, so that's nice. Its scenes with little girl Stellar are rather cute, if not a bit out of place, and the costume/creature serves the story well enough. I do feel the story itself is a bit thin but, again, I'm not too bothered - I just get whisked away in the show's newfound energy and freshness. Slightly more suspension of disbelief may be required during Season 24 (and especially in Dragonfire) but that's not an issue for me; I believe the production team made the best of what they had. There's a sense of effort being put in, even with the seemingly harsh budgetary limitations and the crappy attitude of the Beeb surrounding the show at this point in the 80s.

Dragonfire finishes up a run of just four (struggling) serials and, if you ask me, performs admirably. Give me this new slightly flimsy but breezy and fresh season over the grim and miserable Season 22 any day of the week. The BBC apparently wanted a radical shake up and they got it, and I, for one, love it. At last, the show presents a feeling of joy again, with a whimsical Doctor just enjoying his adventures. A weight's been lifted, Cartmel's arrived, and the show is starting to evolve and find its feet once again.

Up yours, BBC, Dragonfire is great.

Thursday, 15 May 2014

Jingle Bell Rock

Thursday, 15 May 2014
There isn’t really anything to not like about The Christmas Invasion, in my book anyway. I quite like the way we directly continue on with themes and characters from Series 1; it’s a nice link, a continuity to reassure the viewers they’re watching the same show. Eccleston may be out, but the new chap’s in... he just needs a little lie down first to recharge whilst killer robot Santas and Christmas trees set their sights on him.

There are two major highlights in this episode: Harriet Jones (yes, you know who she is) and the Sycorax. Don’t get me wrong, there’s a lot of other stuff to enjoy as well, but Harriet and the Sycorax (as well as sounding like a band name) are top of the list. Penelope Wilton is a joy to watch and I only wish they’d given her more material during Tennant’s run as the Doctor. Of the early Davies era, Jones is one of my favourite characters and I love the journey she goes on too. It’s interesting, come the end of this story, that we’re left with her standing off against the Doctor. It enables a contrast, to show the Doctor is all new. But it’s fascinating: the Doctor is our hero, yet we’re left feeling sorry for Harriet. She only ever meant well and did right by the human race. The ‘right decision’ debate could rage on eternally, but what’s most interesting is the fact that the Doctor’s arrogance here kicks off a fatal chain of events: Harriet is shunted out of office, paving the way for a new Prime Minister... Mr Harold Saxon... you can join the rest of the dots yourself.

Harriet is a focal point of the episode for the most part, leading UNIT and temporarily standing in for the Doctor. Winton is a fantastic actress, hitting every note perfectly and I’m so glad Russell made her into a semi-recurring character.

Before we get to the alien invaders, let’s focus on Rose and co. Rose has always been intended as the ‘audience identification’ figure and, her debut episode aside, this is possibly the most prominent example. She, like many new viewers, must adapt to this new Doctor. She must accept it, come to terms with it, wrap her head around this change. This may be Doctor Who but it’s very much still Rose’s show, for the time being. The decision to pivot around the Tylers and Mickey is a great choice, holding off a ‘big reveal’ of the new Doctor until the third act. We see Mickey beginning to develop more, having shed his ‘bumbling’ persona in Boom Town, and Jackie is as comical as ever, all providing wonderful contrast to the seriousness of the Christmas day invasion...

...of the Sycorax! One of the best alien designs we’ve seen, and a crying shame they haven’t returned (blink-and-you’ll-miss-it cameo in the Underhenge aside) for a proper rematch. They’re developed enough for the episode, and several hints are given as to their civilisation and culture – it sounds fascinating. A far cry from some aliens we’ve seen in the show. Perhaps the only real flaw is that they appear to have buttons which, once hit, open the floor of their spaceship in plot-convenient places. The stand-off between human and Sycorax is also a highlight, especially as we witness Rose trying to address the Sycorax using some terminology and phrases she’s heard in her travels with the Doctor: as strong as she is, she still needs the Doctor, but she’s not afraid to stand in his place and have a bloody good go. And then we get the “you’re speaking English” moment and the episode ramps up a notch. The punch-the-air moment; the Doctor is in.

The Christmas Invasion never stops feeling confident. In one sense, it’s a risky move to hold off the big reveal and have the Doctor cooped up in bed for most of the episode, but the pay-off is perfect and the whole thing ties together brilliantly. Russell drags elements from Series 1 forward, giving everything a polish, and paves the way for the new era to begin. Series 2 may not be the most highly-regarded or best series but you can’t deny it begins on a real high.

Moonbase Under Siege

Wednesday, 7 May 2014
Early Patrick Troughton! The first reappearance of the Cybermen! Ben and Polly and Jamie! It’s all going on with this one. The Moonbase has been on my shelf for a while and I finally got round to watching it last night. This is the third time I’ve experienced DVD animation (after The Invasion and The Tenth Planet) so I’m used to it, though kicking off the serial with it was definitely a little jarring and, due to episodes one and three being missing, it’s a constant back and forth throughout the story. Still, it was very well done and kudos to the animation team. (One day I may get around to viewing telesnaps and recons but for now I’m fine with animation.)

It’s now about fourteen weeks since William Hartnell was killed by the Cybermen and Patrick Troughton is now very much settled into the role. We’re four stories into his era and he’s comfortably Doctor Who. Also, arguably, this serial acts as a pivot for the show – for the first time, the Doctor explains that there are evils in the universe and they must be fought. He’s still a wanderer, an adventurer, but he has ‘duties’ to do, he’s a sort of ‘caretaker of the universe’, righting wrongs and sorting out evils (an idea brought up again later – “all these evils I have fought” – in his trial at the end of The War Games). Amusingly, however, Troughton gives this speech and then flits about in the background for a lot of the story. The director, Morris Barry, has told Troughton to turn his performance down and Serious Business Troughton is a delight to watch when he finally gets some dialogue and action. Here’s where I don’t think the animation (or indeed any form of reconstruction) can really do the live action justice. They try to capture Troughton’s mannerisms but oh how I wish we could see him properly. Even in duff or slow scenes, he’s constantly watchable.

I also love, in episode three, when we hear the Doctor’s internal monologue (pre-recorded speech played over the scene) and how he replies to it. Such a scene is very rare in Doctor Who – I don’t think it’s happened since – and, again, it’s really well done by Troughton. Just a shame the live action clip doesn’t exist (a phrase to be constantly repeated throughout the 60s, sadly).

Anyway. Three’s a crowd and, with the team having had to rewrite some of their material to fit new boy Jamie in, he unfortunately is put out of action for the story whilst Ben and Polly do most of the legwork. Not bad for Frazer though, as he admits he was totally fine with being paid to lie on a bed and be looked after by Anneke Wills! Speaking of whom, Polly is the one who comes up with the nail polish remover stuff which ultimately helps in defeating the Cybermen. Sure, she’s told to stay behind by Ben (“this is men’s work”) and the Doctor asks her to make coffee, but it’s this act which enables her to then kick off the chain of events leading to a solution. I haven’t seen enough Ben and Polly to know whether this is a rare moment in which she gets to shine, or whether she’s given more material like this in other stories, but I’m glad Kit Pedler actively gave the companions some good material here.

And so we reach the Cybermen! Back for Round 2 with shiny new costumes, pointy hands, accordion chest units and unnerving new voices. I particularly love the way they’re introduced in episodes one and two, with just one of them creeping and lurking around the base in the shadows. The Cybermen don’t even speak until episode three, and then they impressively appear on the lunar surface in rather a large quantity. I also quite like that the characters already know of the Cybermen (“every child knows”) and how the events of The Tenth Planet are directly referenced. I have to admit the voices irritated me a little (and it was sometimes hard to make out what they were on about) but they were highly effective, very creepy, and they fit these new more-robotic Mondasians to a T. Whilst I love the sing-song voices of those in The Tenth Planet, they just wouldn’t feel right coming from this bunch, with their fully metal helmets and slit mouths. All-in-all, this is a very strong return for them. As with the Daleks in their second appearance, it’s very easy to see why they were then brought back time and time again.

If I had to rate The Moonbase out of 10, it’d probably be a 7. It’s got its flaws (Kit Pedler is a scientist, you say?) and it may be a little ‘thin’ in terms of the story, but it has a great atmosphere, the Cybermen are great and it’s a very solid production with some great turns by everyone in the cast. (They even throw a silly Doctor Who hat into the mix, along with a rather bizarre ‘time scanner’ which is cliffhanger-convenient and never seen again, much like the Time-Space Visualiser.) At four episodes it never outstays its welcome and it’s well worth a watch, if only for the Cybermen and early-era Patrick Troughton.

Terry Nation Strikes Again!

Friday, 2 May 2014
‘I am Dalek Dave, your tour guide for the day.’
I adore the black and white era of Doctor Who. Alongside the Cartmel and Davies eras, it’s my favourite. And, I have to say, I also prefer my Daleks in black and white too. The Daleks, even the souped up Bronzies and the glorious Imperial Whites, never quite look as effective in colour. Which is interesting because The Dalek Invasion of Earth was made in black and white and then again in colour (albeit in movie format with a different cast). I’ve only seen this story twice but I can comfortably say it’s one of my favourite Hartnells, one of my favourite 60s’ serials, and one of the best Dalek adventures of the entire 50-year history. If you disagree, you’re just wrong.

I recently ‘live-tweeted’ the 6-part epic, often pausing to make comments, both silly and serious. And, as I acknowledged whilst doing so, it’s worth noting that the show was not made for this in mind. Indeed, the way I watched it is totally at odds with the intention. It was made to be watched just once, in 1964, one episode a week. In both of my viewings, I watched it all in one go (‘tis how I prefer to watch the old stories, treating them like movie-length epics with cliffhanger beats)... but both times I outright adored it.

There’s a whole Bedfordshire mine full of production issues and ‘bloopers’, but the vast majority of these we can let slide due to the context; the incredibly small budget and the timeframe in which it was produced. 

So instead let’s focus on the story and probably the first thing to note is the Daleks’ main plan – it’s just downright bizarre (the whole thing is just guff). But, again, since we’re riding the wave of Dalekmania, this can also be allowed to slide. Because we’re watching for the Daleks more than anything; even the regulars seem a little ‘secondary’ in nature, though they’re all on fine form as usual. Hartnell especially. In fact, he seems to step up a notch come the cliffhanger of World’s End (episode one). To be honest, the first episode is just a prolonged faff about until the Daleks show up [their return was known by viewers in advance at the time]. Nation even throws in the Robomen to fill some time as we wait. But then, when that Dalek emerges from the Thames (what was it doing down there anyway, having a bath?), the foot slams onto the pedal and we begin to race into whole new territory.

The Doctor, upon seeing the Dalek, faces it down immediately and starts to show glimpses of ‘the hero’ we all know the character as today. This may only be the Daleks’ second appearance in the show, but they’re definitely his big enemy now and Hartnell plays with this dynamic brilliantly. Further into the story (before he goes on holiday and misses a chunk of the middle), he gets a brilliant scene in the Dalek cell, proving himself to be highly intelligent and crafty, and then, come the climax, he takes great delight in helping Barbara to recondition the Robomen and change their orders. 

Prior to my rewatch of The Dalek Invasion of Earth, I’d always considered Hartnell’s defining moment of ‘change’ into ‘the Doctor’ we all recognise to be the moment he triumphantly faces up to Koquillion in The Rescue, but I think the ‘change’ actually begins here, one story earlier. In any case, it’s a gradual process, and The Dalek Invasion of Earth showcases the first major glimpse of the Doctor’s growth as a character and as a person. This is rather a large turning point for him, and Hartnell is now clearly extremely comfortable and confident in the role. Being Dr Who is now a breeze for him, it seems, and I punched the air each time he appeared following an absence.

Hartnell’s Dr Who is also given a new lease of life, particularly in terms of his relationship with Susan. They share a number of scenes, but Susan is paired off with David for most of the story and, as they grow close, the Doctor notices from afar. Ultimately, he makes the decision for her, and deems her to be ‘a woman now’, no longer in need of ‘a silly old buffer’ like him. He ultimately shunts her out of the TARDIS so she can ‘belong somewhere’ (something she’s always dreamed of), and the Doctor becomes the ‘lone wanderer’ for the first time. He still has Ian and Barbara, of course, but his grand-daughter is gone and the show’s first ever companion has departed. Hartnell is clearly affected by this, and his Doctor now has to change and develop. That closing scene and the speech is now extremely iconic, but it packs far more of a dramatic punch viewed in context, after six episodes of Dalek goodness.

His decision to simply leave Susan is a highly debatable topic, even to this day, but I’m of the opinion that it’s great. It’s problematic – how can the Doctor say he’ll come back one day, knowing full well he has no TARDIS control, for a start? – but it’s so well played by both Hartnell and Ford that you just get totally wrapped up in the emotion of the scene and ride along with it all. Even with the two visibly separated, in two different sets, it’s an extremely powerful scene and one of Doctor Who’s finest hours.

And then for the TARDIS to fade and the camera to linger with Susan and David... what a brilliant decision. The Doctor really is gone, even away from the viewers, and Susan takes hold of David’s hand as they walk off to rebuild London. Instead of cutting to the Doctor to close the episode (as is often the norm), I love that we instead end the story with Susan and her dropping of the TARDIS key. For those who say the companion leaving scenes of older Who are crappy, you only need show them this one – the very first one – to prove them wrong. It’s just gorgeous, and I know I’m fawning over it but it deserves it. (It’s then also wonderfully followed up at the start of The Rescue, with the Doctor temporarily forgetting Susan is gone and Barbara having to comfort him.)

The Underwater Menace...
The above just shows how easy it is to skip directly to the closing moments of Flashpoint. It’s a scene that can be viewed time and time again and never get old. But as I noted about the speech, it’s best viewed after having watched the 6-part build-up. Because what a build-up it is! Daleks, Robomen, an invaded Earth, iconic shots of London, explosions, mines, and a force to be reckoned with – the Slyther. (Still, it beats a giant clam, doesn't it?)

The adventure constantly keeps moving, making it rather easy to swallow in one sitting should you choose that option, and it’s full of neat little touches. The VETOED signs, the (presumably) Dalek insignia, the rebels, and, of course, the expansion of Dalek mythology. Here, we get a Supreme Commander and a Black Dalek, along with a Dalek saucer and the ‘middle period’ of Dalek history (which is all very wonky, but go with it), along with Sky dishes stuck to their backs. The Daleks also receive bigger ‘dodgem’ bumpers, elevating their height and bulkiness, evolving them into something bigger than before. And, honestly, it’s all rather good stuff... apart from the awkward voices which, as Barbara proves, sound like they’re simply muffled human voices (or ‘hold your nose and talk’ voices). It’s a shame but it does provide for a variety of voices, which must be seen as a positive.

The Robomen, perhaps, don’t work quite as well. They always sound like they’re runnningggg outttt of battttterrrrriessss and they’re a bit cumbersome. But the concept behind them is a great one – in effect, they’re proto-Cybermen two years before Kit Pedler and Gerry Davis arrive on the scene – and they are better than the Slyther (though, wisely, director Richard Martin doesn’t linger on the whole creature for too long). With the story focus being very much on the Daleks, however, it doesn’t matter that the secondary monsters are a bit pants.

The underground rebels are rather good too, and particular credit must go to Peter Fraser (David), Ann Davies (Jenny) and Alan Judd (Dortmun) for their constantly brilliant performances throughout. Often overlooked, I think, are also Jean Conroy and Meriel Hobson, who play the two women in the woods who work for the Daleks. They're both deliciously creepy and steal that whole scene.

What lingers in the memory most, however, is surely the imagery. (Note: It is forbidden to dump bodies in the river.) The deserted London; the Daleks prowling around various iconic landmarks; and the dash across London with Barbara and co.

The Skarosian pepperpots on Westminster Bridge is the Dalek image of the Hartnell era, if not the 60s. The entire serial feels epic, even ‘blockbuster’ if you like. And, as per my opening statements, the serial being black and white really adds to the atmosphere. Compare to the opening episode of Invasion of the Dinosaurs; deserted London in colour is great, but my god it’s so much more effective in B&W.

And as if all that wasn’t enough, Terry Nation caps the main story off in style with huge volcanic eruptions in England. Even the Doctor can’t quite believe it!

The Dalek Invasion of Earth is just incredible from start to finish, and really cements the Daleks as the foe of the show. After this, you just know they’re going to return again. They have to, and rightly so. Admittedly, the serial is helped by Susan’s departure scene, but that’s just the icing on the already delicious cake. So go and watch it if you haven’t already, or go and watch it again if you have. Plunge yourself head first into the story and be whisked away by the pure joy and excitement of it all! (Totally not sorry.)

And I didn't even mention the bastards of Earth, the jolly good smacked bottom or the pie-dish spaceships.

Look Who’s Back!

Thursday, 1 May 2014
Mr Ollis jumps the queue
Welcome, one and all, to the Mystical Wonder World of Omega. Please make your way through the jelly corridors, following the Gell Guards, and enjoy the looped ‘jingle-jingle-da-da’ music as you wait in line. The wait time is currently 25 minutes, approximately, so please hold tight. Upon your arrival in Omega’s HQ, please take a seat on the throne and await further instructions. Customers should also note that all water dispensers, laboratory doors and yellow cars should remain outside the palace. We hope you enjoy the ride.

Since it’s a bit of a special occasion for the Doctor Whos, they’re all invited back for a little party. No former companions though, no, no. Just a comedy chap called Mr Ollis (who is, to be fair, brilliant – the way the whole serial is bookended with him is great) and a scientist called Dr Tyler who’ll literally give us some padding and then outright tell us, ‘that was a bit of a waste of time.’ Intentional in-joke by Bob Baker and Dave Martin there? I wouldn’t put it past them, especially considering the sense of fun this serial shoots for. Fun mixed with a bit of panto, mixed with jelly.

The Brigadier’s also invited to the party but he seems to have mislaid his usual persona or undergone a personality transplant with Benton. Their roles appear switched – the Brigadier gets all the ‘comedy’ lines, but frankly comes off as a bit of a div, whereas Benton takes everything in his stride. Mind you, the Brig isn’t the only character who seems a little different...

Patrick Troughton’s back! But he seems to be playing ‘the Second Doctor’ as opposed to ‘the Doctor’. He seems to be a bit of a parody of his former self, and is made to look a bit foolish, actually, with the constant upset over his recorder. It’s as though the writers have remembered a couple of things about Troughton’s incarnation and then exaggerated on them a bit. It’s fine, ultimately, because Troughton pulls it off (and it’s Patrick Troughton! In colour!) but still a touch jarring. At least he’s out of the jar though, unlike William Hartnell. More of a triangle really. Again, it’s quite hard to recognise his Doctor through the illness and the ‘cue card acting’ – not to mention he’s misguidedly written as ‘the wise old man’ (though admittedly it’s not as bad here as it is in The Five Doctors) – but, as with Troughton, you can overlook it because, hell, it’s Hartnell back on screen. And he is, sadly, quite ill.

In my interview with writer Bob Baker, he notes how Hartnell’s part was ‘cut to a bare minimum’, which is unsurprising due to his deteriorating health. Beyond the TV screen Hartnell finds himself trapped in, however, you can see the enthusiasm and glee bubbling away, even though he can’t quite manage it. How lovely that he got to return to the series as Dr Who one more time!

Give us a wave!
So the party’s all set, even if the decorations are a bit suspect (Omega’s HQ is bloody shoddy – I know he has to muster it all up using his will but come on, he could at least will up some curtains and drapes and luxury. Maybe he’s too focused on the jelly.) and all the guests arrive. 

Even the production team get a brief cameo (see right)!

So what of the pre-drinks? It all kicks off at UNIT HQ where Mike Yates seems to be taking a holiday and, for a change, the Master has fucked off for a bit. Dr Tyler is processing some scans (you can’t fool me; you’ve been sat on the photocopier again, haven’t you!?) whilst twiddling some knobs, as a dodgy anti-matter creature is on the prowl like Richard Hillman at Maxine’s. Said creature then gobbles up the party-goers one by one, transporting them to Springwell Quarry in Hertfordshire (aka the Mystical Wonder World of Omega) for the main event. It’s quite a good set-up, particularly as the Doctor Who rulebook is torn up and shredded in Episode One. We begin like any other Pertwee story but things very quickly take a dramatic turn. Even the Time Lords, who are too boring to party, are in danger as their lights in Groovy Control go down.
During the Bessie ride home at the end of the night, one COME WHINE WITH oMEga  attendee had this to say: ‘10/10 for the casting of Clyde Pollitt as the Time Lord Chancellor, bringing a nice sense of continuity between this and The War Games.’
So, after fannying about with more Gell Guards, forcefields and oh-who-cares-anymore, we finally get to meet the host of the party. This is the bit we’ve all been waiting for. The man behind the wibbly wobbly guardy wardies, the man lodged up his own black hole, the man in the mask... it’s Omega. Pronounced: ‘I AM OHHHHMEGA!’ And Stephen Thorne is excellent. If you thought seeing Hartnell, Troughton and Pertwee together (well, kind of) was exciting, you’re in for another treat with Ohmygod Omega. 

Interestingly, Thorne slowly descends Omega into madness over time (rather than booming like BRIAN BLESSED from the start), and come Episode Four he’s really ramped up the volume (it’s clearly way past midnight and he doesn’t give a shit about the neighbours). He’s clearly had a bit too much to drink too as he tumbles and falls in a brawl with Pertwee. But then the lights go down, the mask comes up, and Omega simply becomes ‘Oh’. (I’d try and explain the science [note: impossible] but we’re all far too high on life to care at the moment. Anti-matter? Matter? Doesn’t ruddy matter.) Omega’s lack of physical being is a great plot twist, only slightly ruined by the fact we can see Thorne’s face through the mask, but it’s well realised and really effective.

Omega’s just faultless, in terms of how he’s written and how he’s portrayed. Whilst the celebration may be a little bit of a shambles and all a bit cheap, the acting on display throughout all four episodes is phenomenally good (even Nick Courtney with his unfortunate dialogue), and kudos to Bob and Dave for creating this mad man. The stakes are also quite massive in terms of the threat, without it ever feeling over-the-top, and each episode has something tasty. If you imagine The Three Doctors as a party food spread, it’s like that: some really cack food, but then you spot the massive chocolate cake and the extra goodies hidden in amongst the other offerings. There’s even a balloon for you, right at the start of Episode One!

Unable to control his excitement,
Mr Ollis lets one rip in the console room
I can only imagine what viewers thought at the time, seeing the other Dr Whos back in the TARDIS alongside Pertwee, and the whole serial proved to be quite a treat. I mean yes, there is some shoddy production and the plot’s a bit muddled and the resolution is downright weird (how the fuck did that recorder conveniently fall right into the middle of the console device?), but it’s still great fun. We have to remember the early shows were designed to be watched just the once, not to be analysed and viewed in-depth repeatedly some 40 years later... 

So, for what it is, it’s a delight. It’s a bit hammy and panto (it aired just after Christmas in ‘72, mind you) but it has some wonderful ideas, builds on Time Lord mythology, paves the way for the future (Doctors can come back, and Pertwee can now travel properly using the TARDIS) and introduces one of the best villains to the show. Marvellous stuff.

One day, he will come back. Yes, he will come back.

Wednesday, 23 April 2014

All That Hopping!

Wednesday, 23 April 2014
[Note: There are two versions of “Born Again” out there in the wild. The R2 DVD release saw a ‘rough cut’ version accidentally released, rather than the finished product. For this review, I will be watching the ‘correct’ version as seen on Children in Need 2005 and on the R1 release. Noteworthy, however, is the fact that the rough cut has some very unusual but neat choices in incidental music.]

“Back to your mother’s? I don’t wanna go!”
For the first time ever in Who history, on 18th November 2005, we (along with David Tennant) are treated to a new Doctor’s ‘regeneration scene’ prior to his first full episode to be broadcast. 

Having seen just a taster of Tennant months previously, this is the first proper ‘first look’ at the new chap. What’s important about this is the fact that, come his first proper episode, he spends a lot of the time absent from the main plot; the entirety of The Christmas Invasion is about the build-up to his grand return, and so this minisode acts as a sneak preview, an audition scene, if you like, for everyone. It’s fitting, too, that the minisode begins with a brief recap of the regeneration, solidifying for a whole new generation of viewers that, yes, this is the same show and the same character. It bridges The Parting of the Ways and The Christmas Invasion rather neatly.

There are nods back to Rose, as well as mentions of nanogenes, Captain Jack and Slitheen, and one could view this as Russell T Davies giving one final look back to Series 1, as Series 2 is now on the horizon. The Doctor can’t change back. Christopher Eccleston is gone. He’s David Tennant now, gurning and hopping around the place. We’re heading back to the Powell Estate – same Rose, same TARDIS, same everything... except there’s a new boy in Eccleston’s jacket.

So, Tennant. The new chap. He gets to showcase quite a mad and out-of-control Tenth Doctor. ‘The regeneration’s going a bit wrong,’ he says, ramping up the speed of the TARDIS as they head for the Powell Estate. But, just prior to this, he gets a few moments of sanity, some great conversation (and a humorous dig at Jackie) with Piper’s Rose. Billie really sells her character’s confusion and upset, asking if he can change back, and it’s worth remembering that, at this point in the show’s history, we’re still very much watching through Rose’s eyes. This is very much Rose’s narrative; the outsider looking in once more. And back to Tennant, who looks very much at home zipping around the console (Christopher who?). All this seven-minute minisode does is prove to the world that David Tennant is the right choice. If Euros Lyn’s direction could talk, it’d constantly be saying, ‘just look at him!’

As Doctor Who’s first proper minisode, it’s brilliant. There’s a confidence to it all the way through. And the energy, the atmosphere... and they even get decent TARDIS lighting for a change! Born Again is perhaps not something one could rate out of ten but the job it sets out to do, well, it does it with aplomb. As a bridging scene, it's marvellous. (There’s even a cheeky reference to the weakness in his right hand, an appendage which is shortly about to get the chop.)

And all too soon, it’s over. The cloister bell, last heard in 1996, echoes through the console room as the TARDIS hurtles towards Earth... there’s a Christmas surprise for Jackie and Mickey, but probably not the one they’re expecting!

Crediting Those Behind the Masks

Saturday, 12 April 2014
Over on the Gallifrey Base forums, a member posted a topic about the Kandyman. His question was, ‘Should the Kandyman have been a more human creation?’ Those who know me well enough will know I’m a huge fan of Mr K so this question drew me in.

I was torn. I love the Kandyman as is – he’s deliciously surreal and bizarre and creepy – but I do think it would’ve been interesting had they gone with the original idea of having a human figure instead. I’m picturing a Willy Wonka-esque nutter, perhaps in a lab coat draped in sweets. And, just as a bonus, imagine if said actor was Gene Wilder. I think that’d be glorious.

In any case, I love the Kandyman. And, further into the thread, we started discussing how, sometimes, we tend to forget or gloss over actors in the costumes. We often talk about recognisable people (Michael Wisher and Terry Molloy, for example) but sometimes we forget to acknowledge or really credit those people who sweated like crazy in the unforgiving costumes. All those Silurians, Sea Devils, Zygons, Daleks, Cybermen, Mandrels, Sontarans... and, of course, the Kandyman himself – Mr David John Pope.

When I first bought the DVD of The Happiness Patrol, I fell in love with the story immediately. For a while, I hailed it as my favourite Doctor Who story. It’s definitely still up there (I only wish there was an extra episode, with more Kandy screentime). During my period of Kandymania, I decided to head over to eBay and, once there, I purchased a Kandyman ‘Harlequin’ figure (see left). It was already painted, and I wanted to display it proudly on my DVD shelf.

So I won it, I paid, and it arrived... but in with the figure, in the package, was a photo of the Kandyman which had been signed by David John Pope himself. At first, I thought it was a mistake. Maybe I’d been sent something that wasn’t mine (after all, many people flog autographs and photos on eBay separately), so I contacted the seller to ask. Turns out the seller was none other than David John Pope himself (!) and he’d sent the autographed photo as a bonus little extra, free of charge. I was speechless and overjoyed! I messaged him back, thanking him, and I told him how I adored the Kandyman and the story he featured in.

Looking back, I’m still a little speechless. It was totally unexpected and a wonderful surprise, and it made me realise that there’s far more than just what we see on screen. Behind every heavy costume and ill-fitting rubber mask, there’s a person, an actor, and I don’t think they always get the praise and credit they deserve. We rightfully whoop and cheer the writers, directors, producers and leading actors, but let’s not forget the rest of the gang too.

So here’s to David John Pope, and to everyone else behind those costumes and masks for the sake of giving us some wonderful Doctor Who!

Six Months Since Salamander and Silverstein

Friday, 11 April 2014
Six months ago today, a very special announcement was made regarding Doctor Who. The majority of two Patrick Troughton serials (The Enemy of the World and The Web of Fear) had been found and they were to be released immediately on iTunes, with DVDs to follow. For me, that was the show's real 50th anniversary treat. I'd been following the missing episodes rumours for a while and I was always hopeful... and then it happened. Two stories (bar episode 3 of Web) were back in the archives!

The Two Doctor Whos
I was at my student flat when the announcement arrived. My internet connection was shocking but I stayed up until the early hours of the morning installing iTunes and downloading the episodes. It was like Christmas come early and I recall watching The Enemy of the World that same morning, all in one sitting (it's how I prefer to devour my Who).

Prior to watching, I knew next to nothing about the story. I knew of Salamander and that it featured no aliens, but that was it. And, basically, it blew me away.

Before the recovery, only episode 3 existed in its entirety and a lot of fandom's opinions were based on that. Episode 3, it has to be said, definitely doesn't represent the story as a whole. It's still good, but it essentially acts as a mid-story "breather". And so, after the story was released, it rightly went through a lot of re-evaluation and I even recall many people saying it was far better than they expected, even trumping the already-cemented classic that was The Web of Fear.

Patrick Troughton, in both stories, is the real highlight. In Enemy, he plays four characters (the Doctor, Salamander, the Doctor impersonating Salamander, and Salamander impersonating the Doctor), each one subtly different and each one a delight to watch. It's somewhat harder to judge him in Web, due to him being absent during episode 2 and then episode 3 being a static recon, but he still lights up the story and throws in bundles of energy. The only really unfortunate thing about all of this is that, within the newly found episodes (including The Underwater Menace ep2), there are a lot of very subtle gestures, facial expressions and nuances in Troughton's performance... and, as such, we now know the recons don't quite showcase everything, leaving us to ponder just what other little gems we can't fully appreciate.

Are we there Yeti?
Also worthy of note is the fact that The Web of Fear is the first appearance of Lethbridge-Stewart. Again, it's unfortunate that he appears in the episode we still don't have (and that his first meeting with the Doctor takes place off-screen) but it's still great that we can now enjoy the soon-to-be-Brig's first adventure. Indeed, it's also fun to see a story in which Lethbridge-Stewart is just as much a suspect as everyone else - I can only imagine being a viewer at the time because some of that mystery is lost in hindsight, now that we know he returned to the show many times as an ally of the Doctor and highly-respected member of UNIT.

I can't quite believe it's six months since the announcement. Time flies! I'm now just keeping my fingers crossed that there's more to come somewhere along the line. I recall thinking, when Galaxy 4 ep3 and The Underwater Menace ep2 were announced in 2011, that it could just be a lucky one-off. I never expected more to ever be found... and then these two stories came along at once! It's completely mad. So let's keep our fingers and toes crossed that we can strike 97 down even further in the future.
